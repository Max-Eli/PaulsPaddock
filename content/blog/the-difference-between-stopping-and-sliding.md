Your brakes are the most important safety system on your car. When most people think about brakes, they think about pads and rotors, and those matter. But the fluid that connects your foot to the calipers plays just as big a role in how well the car stops, and it's the part of brake maintenance that gets skipped most often.

## What brake fluid actually does

When you press the pedal, you're not physically squeezing the brakes. You're pushing fluid from the master cylinder through the brake lines to the calipers, where it forces the pistons out and clamps the pads onto the rotors.

Liquid doesn't compress, so the force from your foot arrives at all four wheels instantly and evenly. That only works as long as the fluid stays a liquid and stays clean.

## Why old brake fluid is a problem

Most cars use glycol-based fluid, rated DOT 3, DOT 4 or DOT 5.1. It's hygroscopic, which means it absorbs moisture from the air. That happens slowly, through hoses, seals and the reservoir cap, every day the car exists. Mileage has very little to do with it. Time does.

Water in the fluid causes two problems:

- **A lower boiling point.** Fresh DOT 4 fluid has to resist boiling up to at least 230°C (446°F). Once it has absorbed around 3.7% water, the standard only requires 155°C (311°F). Hard braking heats the fluid, and if it boils it forms vapor bubbles. Vapor compresses, so the pedal goes soft and travels further, right when you need the brakes most.
- **Corrosion inside the system.** Water corrodes the metal parts the fluid touches: brake lines, caliper pistons and the ABS unit. An ABS module costs a lot more than a fluid flush.

## How often to change it

Most manufacturers recommend replacing brake fluid every two years, regardless of mileage. BMW is one of them, and on most BMWs the service reminder will tell you when brake fluid is due.

Florida's humidity doesn't do your fluid any favors. If you track the car or do a lot of hard braking, change it more often. Many track drivers refresh their fluid before every event.

## Signs your brake fluid is overdue

- The pedal feels soft or spongy, or sinks further than it used to.
- The brakes feel weaker after several hard stops in a row.
- The fluid in the reservoir looks dark brown instead of clear to light amber.
- Your service indicator says brake fluid service is due.
- You can't remember the last time it was done, or you bought the car without records.

Color is only a rough guide. The reliable approach is to test it or change it on schedule.

## What a proper brake fluid service involves

A real flush pushes fresh fluid through every line and caliper until all of the old fluid is out, then bleeds the system so no air is left behind. Topping up the reservoir is not the same thing. It just mixes new fluid with old.

## The bottom line

Pads and rotors are what you hear about. Fluid is what makes them work. If it's been more than two years, it's due.
